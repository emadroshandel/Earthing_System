Soil investigation and model

Reduce four-pin field measurements to apparent resistivity, fit a two-, three- or four-layer earth model (IEEE Std 81-2025 §7.3, §7.6), and derive the equivalent uniform resistivity used by the closed-form IEEE 80 equations.

Survey data

Wenner puts the four pins at equal spacing a. Schlumberger keeps the two inner potential pins close together and moves the outer current pins out, so the spacing entered is AB/2, half the distance between the current pins.
Enter the apparent resistivity if the instrument already reports ρₐ, or the raw resistance R if it reports ohms — the program applies the array formula for you.
m
Distance between the two inner potential pins, which stays fixed while the outer current pins are moved out. It appears in the Schlumberger formula, so a wrong value scales every reading. Only used for the Schlumberger array.
One row per pin spacing you measured, smallest first. At least three rows are needed to fit a two-layer model and six (better eight or more) for three layers; a roughly logarithmic ladder of spacings — 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 16, 20, 30 m — resolves the layers best.

Model options

Automatic fits two layers and, when that fit is worse than 3 % RMS or the curve falls and rises again (H-type) or rises and falls (K-type), also fits three; with nine or more readings and a three-layer fit still worse than 3 %, it also tries four. The third layer is kept only if it lowers the misfit by at least a fifth and passes an F test of its two extra parameters at the 5 % level, so noise is not mistaken for a layer. Three layers need at least six readings and four at least nine; the middle layer's resistivity and thickness are often traded against each other (equivalence), and the result says which combination of them the readings actually fix.
m
Depth of cover over the buried earthing conductors, measured from finished ground level to the top of the conductor. Typically 0.5–0.8 m.
m
How far the ground rods reach below the buried grid, not their total length. Leave 0 if there are no rods. It only affects which layer the model is averaged over.
How the two fitted layers are collapsed into the single ρ that the IEEE 80 closed-form equations need. A grid's current goes about one grid radius deep, however shallow the grid is buried, so the grid-size rule treats the grid as a disc of the same area on the layered soil. Automatic uses it whenever the grid area is known; the grid page's Pull inputs recomputes it for the actual grid. The depth-weighted average suits small electrodes only. Upper layer only is not conservative when ρ₂ is higher than ρ₁.
m²
Area enclosed by the earthing grid. It sets how deep the grid's current reaches and therefore which layer dominates. Leave empty if not yet known — Pull inputs on the grid page recomputes the equivalent resistivity with the grid's own area and length.
A single bad reading — a pin in a stone, a buried pipe, a misread range — drags a least-squares fit towards itself. With this on, the residuals of the fit are scaled by their median absolute deviation; a reading more than three robust standard deviations (and at least 5 %) from the curve is set aside, one at a time, and the model refitted. At most a fifth of the readings can be set aside. The excluded readings are listed so you can re-measure them.
Readings from more than one traverse? Give each row its traverse name (for example N–S, E–W). All rows are fitted together, and each traverse with three or more readings is also fitted on its own to show whether the soil varies across the site.

Cable sheaths — earth-fault return

A substation fed by cable: for an earth fault at the far end the fault current returns partly in the cable sheaths and armour and partly through the soil — only the soil part raises the earth. The sheaths are solved as a ladder between the two end earths and any earthed joints or link boxes, with the faulted core inducing its voltage in every section (ENA EREC S34 Appendix D generalised). The result is the split factor of the cable, the earth current and potential at each end and the voltage of every joint.

Earthing conductor sizing

Thermal sizing to IEEE Std 80-2013 Eq. (37)/(45) and the IEC 60364-5-54 adiabatic equation, plus the minimum sizes required by IEC 60364-5-54 Tables 54.1 and 54.2.

Design current

The current the earthing conductor itself has to carry, which is not always the full site fault current — a grid conductor carries only its share.
s
Time the conductor carries that current. The heating is adiabatic, so the required area grows as the square root of this time.
–
Accounts for the d.c. offset in the first cycles, which heats the conductor more than the a.c. value alone. Take the value computed on the fault-current page; 1.0 ignores the offset.
°C
Ambient soil or air temperature the conductor starts from. A higher starting temperature leaves less room before the fusing limit and increases the area.

Material and joints

Sets the conductivity, fusing temperature and thermal capacity used in the IEEE 80 equation. Copper-clad steel is the usual compromise between cost and theft resistance.
The joint, not the conductor, usually sets the temperature limit. An exothermic weld can go to the copper fusing point; a bolted joint must stay below 250 °C or it loosens and its resistance rises.
Material for the parallel IEC 60364-5-54 adiabatic check, which uses a k factor rather than the IEEE 80 formula.
Where the conductor runs and what covers it. This sets the initial and final temperatures, and therefore k — a bare buried conductor may run hotter than one bundled with cables.
mm²
Cross-section of the line conductor the protective conductor runs with, used for the S/2 and 16 mm² rules of IEC 60364-5-54 Table 54.2 (BS 7671 Table 54.7). Leave 0 if you are not sizing a PE from a line size.
BS 7671 Table 54.1: "protected against corrosion" means a sheath. A bare conductor buried in soil is not protected and must be at least 25 mm² copper or 50 mm² steel, whatever the thermal calculation says; IEC 60364-5-54:2011 (542.3.1, Table 54.1) asks the same 25 mm² of copper, 50 mm² where a lightning protection system is connected, and 10 mm round (78.5 mm²) hot-dip galvanized steel. A sheathed conductor may be 16 mm², or 2.5 mm² copper if it is also mechanically protected.
IEC 60364-5-54:2011 Table 54.1: a bare copper earthing conductor buried in soil must be 50 mm² (not 25 mm²) where the earth electrode also serves a lightning protection system (IEC 62305-3).
Which thermal criterion sizes the conductor (docs/THEORY.md §4). An HV substation grid designed to IEEE 80 is sized by IEEE 80 with the joint temperature, which is where welded joints save copper; an LV protective or earthing conductor is sized to IEC 60364-5-54. Taking the larger of the two is the safe choice when both standards apply or the basis is not yet fixed. The Table 54.1 minimum applies in every case. Record the basis in the design report.

Numerical analysis — boundary-element solver

Arbitrary electrode geometry in uniform or layered soil (up to four layers), or two soils side by side across a vertical boundary. The buried metal is discretised into segments held at a common potential; solving for the leakage-current distribution gives the true earth resistance, the surface potential field and the touch and step voltages everywhere — without the shape restrictions of the closed-form equations.

Soil and current

The soil is then ρ₁ on one side and ρ′ on the other, each uniform to any depth, and the solution is exact (images in the boundary). Use it with ρ₁ only: leave the lower layers blank.

pts
Number of points across the scanned area in each direction, so 61 means the surface potential is evaluated on a 61 × 61 grid. Raise it for a smoother map, at the cost of solve time.
m
The stride length used to evaluate step voltage — the distance between a person's feet. IEEE 80 and IEC 60364 both use 1 m.
The leakage current crowds towards the free end of every conductor — the grid corners, rod tips — and so do the touch and step voltages. With this on, the segment at each conductor end is halved three times towards the end. On the IEEE 80 Annex B grid it gives with uniform 3 m segments the corner touch voltage of a uniform 0.5 m mesh, at a quarter of the unknowns.
%
After the solution, the segments within 3 m of the worst touch and step points (and of their mirror images) are halved and the model solved again, until both voltages change by less than this percentage — 0.5 % is a good value. It removes the local discretisation error where the verdict is decided; the convergence check under Accuracy and soil studies still measures the global one.
Where people can touch earthed metal — equipment, structures, gates — one per line as name: x0, y0, x1, y1 (a rectangle, m), optionally followed by its own limit in volts. With zones the touch voltage is checked only there. Without them it is checked wherever a person standing on the soil is within 1 m of buried metal, or inside a mesh — so the empty notch of an L-shaped grid no longer counts.

Transferred potential

A pipe, rail, cable screen or bare earth wire that leaves the station carries part of its potential rise out (IEEE Std 80 clause 17.3). The conductor has series impedance with earth return and leaks into the soil. A bare or coated conductor is solved together with the station, so its own leakage changes the soil potential round the station and beside the route; an insulated screen or a rail is solved as a lossy line driven by the bond and by the soil potential the station imposes along the route. It uses the soil, current and electrodes above.

Lightning response

The electrodes above under a lightning current, solved in the frequency domain as thin wires in a lossy half-space (the electromagnetic model of the Theory & reference page, §10.7). It gives the harmonic impedance, the potential rise for a first and a subsequent stroke, and the impulse coefficient, in uniform or layered soil, with or without soil ionisation. Concrete foundations are included with their concrete.

Layered: the exact (Sommerfeld) solution of the stratified soil, with ρ₁ … ρ₄ and the thicknesses above; each layer gets the same frequency dependence and ε_r. Uniform: one resistivity, ρ₀ below.
Ω·m
With a uniform soil: the low-frequency resistivity of the soil around the electrode. Blank uses ρ₁ of the soil card.
Used when the soil is taken as constant with frequency; 10 is the usual assumption.
Soil resistivity and permittivity fall with frequency, strongly above about 300 Ω·m. CIGRE TB 781 recommends taking it into account above 300 Ω·m and requires it above 700 Ω·m; constant parameters are conservative.
m
Where the down-conductor meets the electrode. The nearest node of the model is used.
The TB 781 first stroke has the concave front and second peak of measured first strokes; the Heidler one rises smoothly in 8.4 µs. Both subsequent strokes peak in about 0.8 µs.

Accuracy and soil studies

Each study re-solves the electrodes and soil above. The convergence check halves the segments and estimates the converged values. The seasonal study replaces the top of the soil by a dry or frozen layer. The uncertainty study draws soil models from the fitted model's covariance (page 1) and gives the spread of the results and the probability of meeting the limits.

×
How many times more resistive the top soil is at the driest time of year than when it was measured. 1.5–3 is common; IEEE Std 80 §13.4 and IEEE Std 81 §7.3 warn that the survey is one moment of the year.

Depth to which the soil dries out.
Ω·m
Resistivity of frozen top soil, often several thousand Ω·m. Leave blank where the ground does not freeze.
m
Depth of frost penetration.
Number of soil models drawn. Each one is a full solution, so 20 samples take about a minute for a substation grid in layered soil. The result states the 95 % confidence interval of each probability and how many samples would narrow it to ±5 %.
Latin hypercube divides each soil parameter's distribution into as many equally likely bands as there are samples and draws one sample in each band, so even 20 samples cover the tails evenly; the percentiles are steadier than with random sampling for the same number of solutions (McKay, Beckman and Conover, 1979).

Conductor impedance — non-equipotential solution

The solver above holds all buried metal at one potential. A large grid, steel conductors or low soil resistivity break that: the fault current enters at one point and loses voltage along the conductors. This solves the same electrodes at power frequency with the conductors' internal impedance and their self and mutual inductance with earth return and compares the result with the equipotential one. Fences are solved too: their posts and buried wire leak current, the fabric joins them above ground, and a bonded fence is connected to the grid node nearest to it.

Sets the internal impedance. Steel at 50 Hz carries its current in a skin about a millimetre deep and has several times the resistance it has at d.c.
