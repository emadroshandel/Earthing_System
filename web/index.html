Soil investigation and model

Reduce four-pin field measurements to apparent resistivity, fit a two-layer earth model (IEEE Std 81-2012 §8), and derive the equivalent uniform resistivity used by the closed-form IEEE 80 equations.

Survey data

Wenner puts the four pins at equal spacing a. Schlumberger keeps the two inner potential pins close together and moves the outer current pins out, so the spacing entered is AB/2, half the distance between the current pins.
Enter the apparent resistivity if the instrument already reports ρₐ, or the raw resistance R if it reports ohms — the program applies the array formula for you.
m
Distance between the two inner potential pins, which stays fixed while the outer current pins are moved out. It appears in the Schlumberger formula, so a wrong value scales every reading. Only used for the Schlumberger array.
One row per pin spacing you measured, smallest first. At least three rows are needed to fit a two-layer model, and a roughly logarithmic ladder of spacings — 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 16, 20 m — resolves the layers best.

Earth-fault current

Line-to-earth fault current to IEC 60909-0, then the maximum grid current IG = Df·Sf·Cp·3I₀ of IEEE Std 80-2013 clause 15.

Source data

Network + transformer data builds the sequence impedances from the supply short-circuit level. Use sequence impedances if the utility gave you Z₁ and Z₀ directly, or 3I₀ if you already know the earth-fault current.
kV
Nominal line-to-line voltage of the faulted system, not the phase voltage.
–
IEC 60909 voltage factor. 1.1 for maximum fault current above 1 kV — it accounts for the highest operating voltage, tap changers and load flow.
Supply network
MVA
Three-phase short-circuit level at the point of supply. The utility states it on the connection agreement, in MVA or as a fault current in kA.
–
Reactance-to-resistance ratio of the source impedance. It sets how slowly the d.c. offset decays and therefore the decrement factor. 10 is typical for a distribution network, 15–30 near a large generator.
–
Ratio of the zero-sequence to positive-sequence reactance of the source. Around 3 for a typical delta-star transformer; the utility can confirm it.
–
Ratio of the zero-sequence to positive-sequence resistance of the source. 1 is a common default when no better figure is available.
Transformer (optional)
MVA
Rating of the transformer feeding the substation. Leave 0 if you are working straight from the network short-circuit level.
%
Transformer short-circuit impedance from the nameplate, as a percentage of its own rating. Typically 6–12 % for a distribution transformer.
Sequence impedances (Ω)
Ω
Positive-sequence resistance of the source, referred to the fault point.
Ω
Positive-sequence reactance of the source, referred to the fault point.
Ω
Zero-sequence resistance of the source, referred to the fault point.
Ω
Zero-sequence reactance of the source, referred to the fault point.
kA
The total earth-fault current at the site — three times the zero-sequence current. Fill this in to bypass the impedance calculation entirely; leave it blank to let the program compute it.

Grid current factors

s
How long the fault lasts before the protection clears it, including breaker operating time. This sizes the conductor and sets the decrement factor.
s
How long a person could be in contact with the fault. Usually the same as the fault duration, but IEEE 80 lets you use a shorter time where high-speed reclosing means the person is not exposed for the whole event.
Hz
–
The fraction of the earth-fault current that actually leaves the grid into the soil. The rest returns through overhead earth wires, cable sheaths and neutrals, and never raises the site potential. 0.6 means 60 %.
–
Allowance for future growth of the fault level over the life of the installation. 1.0 for no growth, 1.25 to design for a 25 % increase.

Earthing conductor sizing

Thermal sizing to IEEE Std 80-2013 Eq. (37) and the IEC 60364-5-54 adiabatic equation, plus the minimum sizes required by IEC 60364-5-54 Tables 54.1 and 54.2.

Design current

kA
The current the earthing conductor itself has to carry, which is not always the full site fault current — a grid conductor carries only its share.
s
Time the conductor carries that current. The heating is adiabatic, so the required area grows as the square root of this time.
–
Accounts for the d.c. offset in the first cycles, which heats the conductor more than the a.c. value alone. Take the value computed on the fault-current page; 1.0 ignores the offset.
°C
Ambient soil or air temperature the conductor starts from. A higher starting temperature leaves less room before the fusing limit and increases the area.

Material and joints

Sets the conductivity, fusing temperature and thermal capacity used in the IEEE 80 equation. Copper-clad steel is the usual compromise between cost and theft resistance.
The joint, not the conductor, usually sets the temperature limit. An exothermic weld can go to the copper fusing point; a bolted joint must stay below 250 °C or it loosens and its resistance rises.
Material for the parallel IEC 60364-5-54 adiabatic check, which uses a k factor rather than the IEEE 80 formula.
Where the conductor runs and what covers it. This sets the initial and final temperatures, and therefore k — a bare buried conductor may run hotter than one bundled with cables.
mm²
Cross-section of the line conductor the protective conductor runs with, used for the S/2 and 16 mm² rules of IEC 60364-5-54 Table 54.2. Leave 0 if you are not sizing a PE from a line size.
Unprotected buried conductors must meet a larger minimum size to survive corrosion and mechanical damage, whatever the thermal calculation says.

Substation / power-plant earth grid — IEEE Std 80-2013

The complete design procedure of Figure 33: tolerable voltages, grid resistance (Sverak and Schwarz), ground potential rise, mesh and step voltages, and automatic refinement until the design complies.

Site and safety data

Ω·m
Uniform soil resistivity used by the closed-form equations. Take the equivalent value from the soil model page rather than a single field reading.
Picking a material fills in its wet resistivity below. The surface layer is the crushed rock or asphalt on top of the natural soil, not the soil itself.
Ω·m
Resistivity of the surface layer when wet — always use the wet figure, because that is the unfavourable case for a person standing on it.
m
Thickness of the crushed-rock or asphalt layer. 0.102 m is 4 inches, the value used in the IEEE 80 Annex B worked example. Set 0 for bare soil.
s
Duration of the shock a person could receive. It appears under a square root in the tolerable-voltage formula, so halving it raises the limits by about 40 %.
IEEE 80 gives limits for a 50 kg and a 70 kg body. 50 kg gives the lower, more conservative limits and is the usual choice where the public has access.
kA
The part of the earth-fault current that actually flows from the grid into the soil: IG = Df · Sf · Cp · 3I₀. Use “Pull inputs” to take it from the fault-current page rather than entering the raw fault current here.

Grid geometry

m
Overall plan length of the buried grid, edge to edge — the area the conductors enclose, not the length of one conductor.
m
Overall plan width of the buried grid, edge to edge.
m
Centre-to-centre distance between adjacent parallel grid conductors — the size of one mesh. The IEEE 80 equations assume the same spacing both ways, so a 70 m grid at D = 7 m has eleven conductors in each direction. Reducing D is the most direct way to bring the mesh voltage down.
m
Depth of cover from finished ground level to the top of the buried conductors. Usually 0.5 m; deeper burial lowers the touch voltage but barely changes the grid resistance.
m
Diameter of the grid conductor itself, in metres — 0.01 m is a 10 mm round conductor, roughly 79 mm². It enters the mesh-voltage geometry factor only, so it has a weak effect on the result.
Irregular and L or T shapes get the IEEE 80 corrective factor Ki applied to the mesh voltage, because current crowds at re-entrant corners.
Ground rods
–
Total number of vertical ground rods connected to the grid. Rods help most where a low-resistivity layer sits beneath a resistive surface layer.
m
Length of each rod below the grid conductors, not its total driven length.
m
Diameter of each ground rod, in metres — 0.016 m is a standard 16 mm rod.
Rods concentrated on the perimeter and corners are far more effective than rods spread evenly, because that is where the current density is highest.
Sverak is the standard closed form for a grid with or without rods. Schwarz treats the conductors and the rods as two coupled electrodes and is better when the rods dominate. Automatic chooses between them.

Numerical analysis — boundary-element solver

Arbitrary electrode geometry in uniform or two-layer soil. The buried metal is discretised into segments held at a common potential; solving for the leakage-current distribution gives the true earth resistance, the surface potential field and the touch and step voltages everywhere — without the shape restrictions of the closed-form equations.

Soil and current

Ω·m
Resistivity of the upper soil layer. For a uniform earth, put the single value here and leave the lower layer blank.
Ω·m
Resistivity of the lower layer. Leave blank to solve a uniform earth.
m
Depth from ground level to the boundary between the two layers.
A
Current injected into the electrode system, in amperes — note that the grid page asks for the same quantity in kA, so 1.908 kA is 1908 A here.
m
Length each conductor is chopped into before solving. Shorter segments give a more accurate answer and a slower solve; 2–3 m suits a substation grid.
pts
Number of points across the scanned area in each direction, so 61 means the surface potential is evaluated on a 61 × 61 grid. Raise it for a smoother map, at the cost of solve time.
m
The stride length used to evaluate step voltage — the distance between a person's feet. IEEE 80 and IEC 60364 both use 1 m.

Buildings and homes — IEC 60364 / BS 7671

Earth electrode selection, protective conductor sizing, earth-fault loop impedance, maximum disconnection times and RCD selection for TN-S, TN-C-S, TT and IT installations.

Installation

The first letter is how the supply is earthed, the second how the installation is: TN uses a protective conductor back to the source, TT uses its own local electrode, IT has no direct source earth.
V
Nominal voltage between a line conductor and earth — 230 V in a 400 V three-phase system.
Ω·m
Soil resistivity at the electrode location, used to compute the resistance of each electrode you add below.
Final circuits must disconnect faster than distribution circuits: 0.4 s against 5 s in a TN system at 230 V.
Ω
Resistance and reactance of the line conductor from the origin of the installation to the point of the fault. Together with the PE impedance and Z_e it makes up the fault loop.
Ω
Impedance of the protective conductor over the same route back to the source. In a TT system the return is through the soil instead, so this is not used.
Ω
The part of the fault loop outside the installation — the supply transformer and the service cable. The distributor declares it; 0.35 Ω is a common assumed value for a TN-C-S supply.
V
The touch voltage that may be held indefinitely. 50 V a.c. in ordinary dry conditions; 25 V where the standard calls for reduced limits, such as agricultural premises.

Protective device

The device that has to clear the earth fault. An RCD trips on residual current and so does not depend on the fault current being large.
A
For an MCB or fuse this is the rated current in amperes. For an RCD it is the rated residual operating current IΔn, so type 0.03 for a 30 mA device.

Lightning protection earth termination — IEC 62305-3

LPS class parameters, the minimum electrode length l₁ from Figure 3, Type A and Type B earth terminations, down-conductor spacing and the separation distance s.

Structure and LPS

Class I protects against the widest range of stroke currents and demands the most; class IV the least. It comes from the risk assessment of IEC 62305-2, not from this page.
Ω·m
Soil resistivity at the structure, which sets the minimum electrode length l₁ required for this class.
m²
Plan area enclosed by the structure at ground level. It is used to find the mean radius of the ring electrode, re = √(A/π).
m
Perimeter of the structure at ground level, which sets how many down-conductors are needed.
Type B is a ring or foundation electrode round the whole structure and is the normal choice. Type A is separate radial or vertical electrodes, one at each down-conductor, and suits small structures.
m
Diameter of the ring conductor, in metres — 0.01 m is a 10 mm round conductor.
m
Depth of cover over the ring electrode, typically 0.5 m and at least 0.5 m clear of the foundation.
m
Diameter of each vertical electrode in a Type A arrangement, in metres — 0.016 m is a standard 16 mm rod.
m³
Volume of concrete in contact with the earth, if you are using the reinforcement as the electrode. Leave blank to size a ring instead.
Separation distance
m
Distance measured along the down-conductor from the equipotential bonding point up to the level where you are checking the separation — usually up to the nearest metallic service or item of equipment.
What lies between the down-conductor and the metalwork you are separating it from. Concrete and brick need twice the distance of air, because they puncture rather than flash over.

Air termination and protection zone — IEC 62305-3

Where the flash is intercepted, and which volume that interception actually protects. The rolling sphere is rolled numerically over the structure and the terminations, so the result holds for any arrangement; the protective angle and the mesh method are shown beside it for comparison.

Structure

The class sets the rolling sphere radius: 20 m for I, 30 m for II, 45 m for III and 60 m for IV. A smaller sphere reaches into more places, so a higher class is a harder test.
m
Width of the structure measured across the elevation being drawn — the left-to-right direction of the section view.
m
Depth of the structure into the page. It is not used by the elevation; it sets the roof area for the plan-coverage and mesh checks.
m
Height from ground level to the roof surface. Set 0 for open ground, such as a tank farm or a switchyard protected by masts.
m
The surface being protected. Leave blank to protect the roof when there is a structure and the ground when there is not; enter a level to protect something else, such as the top of a tank.
m
Leave blank to use the mesh size the class requires. Enter a value only to test a layout you already have.

Air terminations

x (m)y (m)Height (m)Base (m)
x runs across the section and y into the page, both measured from the centre of the structure. Height is the length of the rod or mast itself; leave the base level blank to stand it on the roof when it falls inside the footprint and on the ground when it falls outside.
Catenary wire
A horizontal wire between two masts intercepts along its whole length rather than at one point, which protects a long object with fewer masts.
m
How far the wire hangs below its supports at mid-span. The sag reduces the protected height in the middle, so it is worth keeping small.

System neutral grounding — IEEE Std 142

Choose between solid, low-resistance, high-resistance, reactance and ungrounded systems; size the neutral earthing resistor; check whether the system is effectively grounded for surge-arrester selection.

System

kV
Nominal line-to-line voltage of the system whose neutral is being earthed.
Hz
km
Total route length of cable connected to the system. Cable capacitance to earth is what makes an unearthed system charge up, so this drives the earth-fault current.
µF/km
Zero-sequence capacitance to earth per phase per kilometre, from the cable data sheet. Around 0.25 µF/km for medium-voltage XLPE.
km
Route length of overhead line. Its capacitance to earth is far smaller than a cable's, so it contributes much less charging current.
kVA
Total rating of motors connected to the system, used for their contribution to the charging current.
kVA
Total rating of transformers connected to the system, used the same way.
Tick this if a single earth fault must not trip the plant. It pushes the recommendation towards high-resistance or unearthed operation, which keeps running with one phase earthed.
Tick this if any load is connected between a phase and the neutral. Such loads need a stable neutral, which rules out high-resistance earthing.
Leave on Recommend automatically to have the program choose from the system data, or force a method to see what it would require.
A
The earth-fault current you want the neutral earthing resistor to let through. High resistance keeps it at or just above the charging current; low resistance is typically 200–600 A to give relays something to see.
s
How long the resistor must carry that current before the protection clears the fault. 10 s is the usual rating; continuous rating costs much more.
Effectively-grounded test (optional)
Ω
Positive-sequence reactance at the point of interest. Fill the three impedances in only if you want the effectively-earthed test for surge arrester selection.
Ω
Zero-sequence reactance at the same point. Effectively earthed requires X₀/X₁ ≤ 3.
Ω
Zero-sequence resistance at the same point. Effectively earthed also requires R₀/X₁ ≤ 1.
